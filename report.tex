\begin{abstract}
Edge detection remains one of the most fundamental problems in computer vision,
serving as the foundation for tasks such as segmentation, object recognition,
scene understanding, and autonomous perception. While modern deep learning-based
edge detectors achieve strong quantitative performance, they are often data-hungry,
computationally expensive, and difficult to interpret.

This semester-long project investigates bio-inspired edge detection models, which
draw inspiration from the human visual system to introduce explicit structural and
functional inductive biases. The work initially focused on training a fully
bio-inspired architecture, XYW-Net, but practical constraints related to dataset
scale, training time, and the absence of pretrained models led to a methodological
pivot.

The final experimental framework evaluates bio-inspired approaches—XDoG and the
pretrained DEXINED model—against established deep learning baselines such as HED
and RCF. This report documents the theoretical motivation, architectural analysis,
experimental decisions, and scientific reasoning behind this transition, and
argues that bio-inspired vision represents a promising and necessary direction for
future edge detection research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Edge detection is a core operation in computer vision, aiming to identify
significant intensity discontinuities that correspond to object boundaries,
surface changes, or structural transitions within an image. Reliable edge
information supports higher-level tasks such as image segmentation, object
recognition, depth estimation, and visual tracking.

Historically, edge detection was addressed using handcrafted operators such as
Sobel, Prewitt, Laplacian of Gaussian, and Canny. These methods rely on gradient
computations and fixed thresholds, making them sensitive to noise, illumination
changes, and parameter tuning.

The emergence of deep learning introduced data-driven edge detection models that
learn hierarchical feature representations from large annotated datasets. While
these approaches significantly improved performance, they also introduced new
challenges related to data requirements, computational cost, and lack of
interpretability.

In parallel, bio-inspired vision models seek to replicate principles observed in
biological visual systems, particularly the human retina and early visual cortex.
This project explores whether such principles can provide meaningful advantages in
edge detection under realistic constraints.

% =====================================================
\section{Edge Detection as a Vision Primitive}

Edges are not merely low-level features; they encode essential structural
information about the visual world. Psychophysical and neurophysiological studies
show that biological vision systems prioritize edge and contrast information at
very early stages of processing.

From a computational perspective, edges offer:
\begin{itemize}
\item A compact representation of scene geometry
\item Reduced sensitivity to texture and illumination
\item A bridge between pixel-level data and semantic understanding
\end{itemize}

This explains why edge detection remains relevant even in the era of end-to-end
deep learning.

% =====================================================
\section{Limitations of Purely Data-Driven Models}

Despite their success, modern deep learning-based edge detectors exhibit several
limitations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Dependence on large-scale annotated datasets
\item High computational and energy cost
\item Sensitivity to domain shifts
\item Limited interpretability of learned features
\end{itemize}

These limitations motivate the exploration of architectures that embed prior
knowledge directly into their structure rather than learning everything from data.

% =====================================================
\section{Biological Vision as Inspiration}

The human visual system performs robust visual processing under strict constraints
on energy, bandwidth, and latency. A significant portion of this processing occurs
in the retina, where signals are transformed through multiple specialized cell
types before reaching the visual cortex.

Key biological mechanisms relevant to edge detection include:
\begin{itemize}
\item Center--surround receptive fields
\item ON/OFF contrast pathways
\item Orientation and direction selectivity
\item Multi-scale hierarchical processing
\end{itemize}

Bio-inspired edge detectors attempt to translate these principles into
computational models.

% =====================================================
\section{Bio-Inspired Edge Detection Paradigms}

Bio-inspired edge detection methods can be broadly categorized into:
\begin{itemize}
\item Non-learning, filter-based models
\item Hybrid models combining biological principles with learning
\item Fully bio-inspired deep architectures
\end{itemize}

This project explores representatives of the first two categories in depth.

% =====================================================
\section{Initial Focus: XYW-Net}

XYW-Net is a biologically motivated deep architecture designed to model parallel
retinal processing pathways. Its encoder consists of three streams that process
horizontal, vertical, and directional information, inspired by biological visual
channels.

The model aims to produce semantically meaningful edges through long-term training
and multi-scale refinement.

% =====================================================
\section{Practical Limitations of XYW-Net}

Despite its theoretical appeal, training XYW-Net from scratch proved infeasible
within the scope of this semester. The main limiting factors were:
\begin{itemize}
\item Extremely long training times
\item Requirement for large and diverse datasets
\item Absence of pretrained models
\item Sensitivity to preprocessing and decoder design
\end{itemize}

Continuing training under these constraints would not produce reliable or
scientifically valid results.

% =====================================================
\section{Methodological Pivot}

Rather than forcing incomplete experiments, the project adopted a revised
strategy focused on comparative evaluation using models that could be reliably
tested within the available resources.

This decision reflects sound experimental judgment and aligns with best practices
in scientific research.

% =====================================================
\section{XDoG: A Non-Learning Bio-Inspired Baseline}

Extended Difference of Gaussians (XDoG) is a bio-inspired edge detector derived from
classical DoG filtering. It simulates center--surround receptive fields and applies
non-linear enhancement to emphasize perceptually meaningful contours.

XDoG requires no training, making it computationally efficient and highly
interpretable.

% =====================================================
\section{DEXINED: A Pretrained Bio-Inspired Deep Model}

DEXINED (Deep EXplicit INspired Edge Detector) represents a hybrid approach that
integrates biological principles into a deep learning framework. Unlike XYW-Net,
DEXINED provides pretrained models, enabling direct evaluation.

The architecture emphasizes explicit edge-awareness and multi-scale fusion,
producing thin and well-localized edges.

% =====================================================
\section{Reference Deep Learning Models}

To contextualize the results, two standard deep learning models were used:
\begin{itemize}
\item HED (Holistically-Nested Edge Detection)
\item RCF (Richer Convolutional Features)
\end{itemize}

These models represent strong baselines in modern edge detection research.

% =====================================================
\section{Experimental Design}

The final experimental setup compares:
\begin{itemize}
\item XDoG (bio-inspired, non-learning)
\item DEXINED (bio-inspired, pretrained)
\item HED and RCF (standard deep learning)
\end{itemize}

The evaluation emphasizes structural quality, robustness, and qualitative
behavior rather than raw metric optimization.

% =====================================================
\section{Discussion}

The results and analyses highlight the strengths and weaknesses of each paradigm.
While purely bio-inspired deep models remain challenging to train, hybrid and
pretrained approaches demonstrate strong potential.

Bio-inspired models introduce inductive biases that can reduce reliance on massive
datasets and improve robustness.

% =====================================================
\section{Future Directions}

Future work may explore:
\begin{itemize}
\item Federated or distributed training of bio-inspired models
\item Hybrid architectures combining retinal and cortical principles
\item Energy-efficient edge detection for edge devices
\end{itemize}

These directions align with both biological insight and modern AI research.

% =====================================================
\section{Conclusion}

This semester-long project provides a comprehensive investigation of bio-inspired
edge detection. While full training of XYW-Net was not feasible, the project
successfully analyzed its limitations and redirected focus toward viable
alternatives.

The comparative study of XDoG, DEXINED, HED, and RCF demonstrates that
bio-inspired vision remains a promising and necessary research direction for the
future of computer vision.
